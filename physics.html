01 · THE STUDY

A daily routine became a research question.

Students carry school supplies from class to class. Our study asks what goes into those bags, how students carry them, what difficulties they report, and which practical changes they would consider.

What were we trying to understand?

School bags can contain notebooks, printed materials, electronic devices, lunch boxes, water bottles, and items for activities such as physical education. Their total mass can change from day to day. The way a bag is lifted and positioned can also change the mechanical demands on the body.

Physics helps describe some of those demands. Weight is a force; lifting a heavier bag through the same height requires more work against gravity; and the distance between a load and a joint can affect torque, or turning effect.

How did we collect information?

The project used a quantitative descriptive survey delivered through Google Forms. Responses were summarized with frequency counts and percentages. The website presents the report’s stated survey total of 26 respondents, as used in its results and updated scope section.

What this method can tell us

It can describe what respondents said they carry, how they report carrying it, and which supports they prefer. It cannot directly measure spinal loading or prove that a bag caused a physical change.

Who might use these findings?

Students can reflect on their packing habits; teachers may consider coordinating materials needed for the day; school administrators can explore secure storage; and future researchers can use these patterns to design studies with direct measurements.

03 · BODY & PHYSICS

The load is not just about kilograms.

Weight, distance, movement, and how a bag sits on the body all matter to the mechanics. Here is the simple version—with a clear line between what physics explains and what our survey measured.

01 · WEIGHT

More mass means more weight.

Gravity pulls down on a bag. If the bag has more mass, the gravitational force is greater. That is why removing items you do not need can make a bag lighter.

Weight = mass × gravity Fg = mg

The survey did not weigh individual bags, so the team cannot calculate each respondent’s bag weight from these responses.

02 · PRESSURE

Force spread over an area.

Pressure describes how much force acts over a given area. A strap’s pressure depends on the force it supports and how that force is distributed across the contact area.

Pressure = force ÷ area P = F / A

The survey did not measure strap force or contact area. This is a concept to help explain—not a measured result.

03 · MECHANICAL WORK

Lifting a heavier bag takes more work.

For the same vertical lift, a heavier bag requires more work against gravity. The height lifted matters too. The same idea explains why putting a bag on a high shelf may feel different from lifting it a short distance.

Work = mass × gravity × height W = mgh

Actual work for respondents would require bag mass and lifting-height measurements.

04 · TORQUE

Distance can change the turning effect.

Torque is the turning effect of a force. If a bag’s weight acts farther horizontally from a body joint, it may create a larger external turning effect. The body responds with muscle activity and adjustments that depend on posture and movement.

Torque depends on force and distance τ = rF sin θ

The survey did not measure joint positions, forces, or moment arms. It cannot quantify spinal torque or disc compression.

So what about spinal loading and posture?

Carrying a load can change the forces and movements involved in standing and walking, and research reviews have examined backpack weight and biomechanics. But this particular survey did not directly measure students’ spinal loading, intervertebral disc compression, or posture. It identifies reported habits and concerns that could guide a more detailed follow-up study.

A PROPOSED SCHOOL-BAG LOAD MANAGEMENT SYSTEM

Three practical parts, working together.

1

Daily packing checklist

Use the timetable to decide what needs to travel with you. Keep necessary personal items; avoid packing “just in case” materials when they are not needed.

2

Classroom storage

Consider secure, accessible storage for materials not needed throughout the day. Any plan should account for space, safety, access, and school rules.

3

Position & handling guide

Adjust straps to fit, keep the load close where practical, distribute contents sensibly, and lift in a controlled way. Ask a trusted adult or school health staff if carrying causes ongoing discomfort.

✦

Comfort matters more than “toughing it out.”

If carrying your bag repeatedly causes pain or difficulty, tell a parent/guardian, teacher, or school health professional. This website offers general education and is not a diagnosis.

What this website is—and isn’t—saying.

How to read the findings

Percentages on this site are drawn from the report’s results. Some questions allowed multiple selections, so item categories may not total 100%. They describe the surveyed group, not every student in Iligan City or the Philippines.

Physics explanations are used to make the topic easier to understand. Where the study did not measure a variable—such as each bag’s mass, strap pressure, joint distance, spinal loading, or disc compression—the website says so rather than presenting an estimate as a result.
