A CONNECTED BIOLOGY STORY · 01

Life begins with light.
What happens when the water changes?

Far below the waves, microscopic organisms turn sunlight into chemical energy and help support aquatic food webs. Meanwhile, tiny pieces of plastic can enter the same environments. To understand why that matters, first meet the life that uses light—then follow what researchers have found (National Geographic Society, n.d.; Zhu et al., 2025).

FIRST, MEET THE INVISIBLE

The ocean's story is not written only by whales and coral. It also depends on life too small to see easily.

Imagine drifting through sunlit seawater. Tiny photosynthetic organisms—including phytoplankton—use light to make energy-rich compounds and help support marine food webs (National Geographic Society, n.d.; Zhu et al., 2025).

Now imagine that the same water contains microplastics: small plastic particles that can enter aquatic environments as larger plastic items break down or through other pathways. Researchers have examined how exposure may influence photosynthesis, but effects depend on context and organism (Zhu et al., 2025).

02 — THE LIFE-MAKING PROCESS

How photosynthesis works

Photosynthesis captures light energy and stores it as chemical energy. In plants, algae, and cyanobacteria, this process links sunlight to food production and the ecosystems around us (National Geographic Society, n.d.).

Light+Water+Carbon dioxide→Sugars+Oxygen

A simplified overview of the inputs and outputs of oxygen-producing photosynthesis (National Geographic Society, n.d.).

01

Capture light

Chlorophyll and other pigments absorb light energy. In plants, chlorophyll is found in chloroplasts (National Geographic Society, n.d.).

02

Use water

Light-dependent reactions use light energy and water, releasing oxygen as part of the process (National Geographic Society, n.d.).

03

Build sugars

The Calvin cycle uses carbon dioxide and energy from earlier reactions to build carbohydrate molecules (National Geographic Society, n.d.).

04

Support life

Stored chemical energy helps organisms grow and supports the food webs connected to photosynthetic life (National Geographic Society, n.d.; Zhu et al., 2025).

03 — WHEN POLLUTION ENTERS THE PICTURE

Small particles.
A much bigger question.

Research has examined how microplastic exposure may influence photosynthesis across terrestrial, freshwater, and marine ecosystems. This study combines observations with model-based estimates, so its global figures should be read as estimates—not direct measurements of every ecosystem (Zhu et al., 2025).

THE QUESTION

What happens when microplastics reach photosynthetic organisms?

Microplastic pollution may affect photosynthetic activity and ecosystem productivity. Effects can vary by organism and environmental conditions, so results should not be assumed to apply equally to every species or habitat (Zhu et al., 2025).

THE EVIDENCE

A wider view across many studies

The PNAS study, “A global estimate of multiecosystem photosynthesis losses under microplastic pollution,” synthesizes existing observations and uses modeling approaches to estimate broader effects (Zhu et al., 2025).

3,286 Observations · 157 studies

What this number tells us

Researchers combined observations with statistical analysis and machine-learning methods to estimate effects across ecosystems. This helps reveal broader patterns, although the findings still depend on the quality and scope of the underlying evidence (Zhu et al., 2025).

UNDERSTANDING THE RESULTS

Read the evidence carefully.

The findings highlight a potential global concern: microplastic exposure may reduce photosynthesis. However, global estimates are based on observations and models. They do not mean every photosynthetic organism or ecosystem responds in the same way.

04 — CONNECT THE SYSTEMS

From a tiny particle to an ecosystem

Photosynthesis is one process within a much larger network. If photosynthetic organisms are affected, consequences may extend beyond the organism itself—but the exact effects depend on the ecosystem and scale of impact (National Geographic Society, n.d.; Zhu et al., 2025).
